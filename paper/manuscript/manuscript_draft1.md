# TITLE
Paired-State Simulation of Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support

# ABSTRACT
Simulators of temporary mechanical circulatory support are increasingly used to reason about device hemodynamics, but few are verified against published data in a way that separates calibration targets from independent evidence. We verified CardioFlow Twin, an open-source, browser-based lumped-parameter simulator that runs two otherwise identical copies of one cardiogenic-shock patient side by side, differing in a single device setting (intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella-class axial pump, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [VA-ECMO], or combinations). The headless solver matched the browser engine to 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁴, time-step halving changed outputs by less than 1.1 × 10⁻⁶, blood volume drifted by at most 2.2 × 10⁻⁹ mL, and paired panes were bit-identical over 1,000 beats. Against three independent porcine studies the model reproduced the direction of 24 of 29 device effects (10 of 11 statistically significant changes); against its source model and calibration anchors, 35 of 38. No device-effect direction reversed under ±20% parameter perturbation or with the baroreflex removed, although removing the reflex doubled ECMO-induced changes (ΔLVEDP +6.5 vs +12.9 mmHg). Impella unloading on ECMO exceeded balloon pumping only above 2.6 L/min of pump flow. Disagreements involved stroke work, stroke volume maintenance, and pulmonary pressure on ECMO. The simulator is verified for directional reasoning about Impella CP-class support and is not validated for patient-specific decisions.

# KEYWORDS
cardiogenic shock; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; intra-aortic balloon pump; computer simulation; ventricular unloading

# INTRODUCTION
Temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) is now a routine part of cardiogenic shock care, yet its hemodynamic effects are difficult to reason about at the bedside. Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) restores systemic flow but raises left ventricular (LV) afterload; transaortic axial pumps unload the LV directly; and the intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) modestly lowers afterload while augmenting diastolic pressure.[@burkhoff2015] Randomized trials have now tested both extracorporeal life support and microaxial flow pumps in infarct-related shock with divergent results,[@thiele2023; @moller2024] which increases the need to understand how each device changes LV loading in an individual circulation.

Closed-loop cardiovascular simulation is a natural tool for this purpose. Real-time models have been used to quantify LV overload during VA-ECMO and to compare unloading strategies,[@broome2016; @donker2019simulation; @donker2019review] and their predictions can be checked against large-animal pressure–volume (PV) data.[@ostadal2015; @hala2020; @mollerhelgestad2019; @frontiers2020impella] However, such simulators are usually reported with their outputs rather than with a verification record, and the published data used to set a model's parameters are rarely separated from data used to test it. When a model is tuned toward a benchmark, agreement with that benchmark is consistency, not validation.

CardioFlow Twin is an open-source simulator that displays two otherwise identical copies of one patient side by side, differing in exactly one device setting, so that the effect of a single intervention can be seen without confounding. Here we report its verification: numerical correctness of the solver, the physiological plausibility of its baseline, directional concordance of simulated device effects with published benchmarks, sensitivity of those directions to parameter uncertainty, and the integrity of the paired-state design. We classify every benchmark as either a consistency source (the model's source model or a calibration anchor) or an independent source, and we report every disagreement.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS
## Model
CardioFlow Twin is a closed-loop lumped-parameter model of the circulation written in TypeScript and distributed as a single HTML file that runs in a web browser (Figure 1). It has seven volume compartments: LV, right ventricle (RV), proximal aortic root, systemic arteries, systemic veins, pulmonary arteries, and a combined pulmonary venous–left atrial compartment. Each ventricle is a time-varying elastance with a double-Hill activation function [CITE NEEDED] and an exponential end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR) with its own volume intercept. Valves are ideal diodes with linear forward resistance. The systemic arterial tree is a three-element Windkessel with a separate proximal aortic node [CITE NEEDED], the venous system is a finite stressed reservoir, and a bronchial shunt connects systemic arteries to the pulmonary veins. Heart rate is fixed at 100 bpm. An arterial baroreflex adjusts systemic vascular resistance (SVR) through a sigmoid of sensed mean pressure with sensing and effector lags of 2 and 8 s [CITE NEEDED]; heart rate, contractility, and venous tone are not reflex-controlled. The equations and full parameter list are in Supplemental Digital Content (SDC) Section S1 and Table S1, and key parameters are in Table 1.

The simulated patient is a single phenotype of low-contractility, afterload-dominant LV failure (end-systolic elastance [Ees] 0.62 mmHg/mL, arterial-to-ventricular elastance ratio ≈4, ejection fraction 16%), representing a Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions stage C–D shock profile [CITE NEEDED]. No normal-physiology parameter set was calibrated.

## Devices
The IABP is modeled as a 40-mL volume displaced into the systemic arterial compartment, inflating at the dicrotic notch and deflating just before aortic valve opening, with timing referenced to an unassisted beat. The Impella-class transaortic pump draws from the LV and returns to the aortic root; its flow lies on a linear pressure head–flow (H–Q) line whose shut-off head scales with the square of a performance level (P1–P9), with a suction roll-off near LV collapse. The H–Q constants are illustrative, chosen so that P2–P8 span approximately 0.5–3.5 L/min, and are not a manufacturer curve. VA-ECMO is an ideal flow source that drains the systemic veins and returns to the systemic arteries, with a drainage roll-off at low venous pressure; it has no centrifugal-pump pressure–flow curve.

## Paired-state design and solver
The application runs two independent instances of the same patient that share one cardiac clock; a single device setting differs between panes. The equations are integrated with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme (1-ms display tick, four substeps; h = 0.25 ms). Each scenario starts from 90 s of unsupported circulation followed by 120 s on the target support, and outcomes are taken from the final beat.

## Parameter provenance
Parameters were set by the developers to reproduce a textbook shock profile. Three groups were adjusted toward published targets during development and are therefore not independent of those sources: the LV EDPVR (A, B, and intercept) was set so that diastolic stiffness matched the change implied by Donker et al. (+10% end-diastolic volume for +5 mmHg wedge pressure at VA-ECMO 4 L/min);[@donker2019simulation] Ees was raised after comparison with the stroke-work direction reported by Ostadal et al.;[@ostadal2015] and the baroreflex was added, with its setpoint chosen to reproduce the pre-existing SVR, to bring the ECMO-induced rise in LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) into a window taken from Donker et al. The baroreflex gain is a modeling assumption, not a measurement; the Donker model itself has no reflexes. No parameter was taken from the independent sources described below, and no parameter was changed for this study.

## Verification
Phase 0 confirmed that a headless runner, which imports the same model source, reproduces the browser engine (tolerance 10⁻⁶). V1 tested time-step convergence (criterion: <0.5% change on halving h), arrival at a periodic steady state, and conservation of total blood volume. V2 compared the unsupported baseline with shock baselines reported in the benchmark sources. V3 compared simulated device effects with published benchmarks. V4 perturbed 15 parameters one at a time by ±20% and removed the baroreflex as a structural alternative, and asked whether any device-effect direction reversed. V5 tested whether changing one pane alters the other.

## Benchmarks
Benchmarks were restricted to open full-text sources that we downloaded and read: the Donker simulation study and its companion review,[@donker2019simulation; @donker2019review] a Broomé and Donker simulation study,[@broome2016] and porcine studies by Ostadal et al.,[@ostadal2015] Hála et al.,[@hala2020] Nix et al.,[@frontiers2020impella] and Møller-Helgestad et al.[@mollerhelgestad2019] The study by Nix et al. was funded by the device manufacturer (Abiomed). The Donker study, its review, the Broomé study, and Ostadal et al. are classified as consistency sources; Hála et al., Nix et al., and Møller-Helgestad et al. were never used in model development and are classified as independent. Every value was transcribed with its table, figure, or text location (SDC Table S5).

Each comparison scored the direction of change of one outcome for one device contrast, matched to the benchmark protocol (for example, ECMO 1→5 L/min for Ostadal et al.; Impella CP at maximum support mapped to P8). Rankings between devices were scored as the sign of the difference in change. Rules were fixed in code before comparison: a model change smaller than 1% was "no change", animal data were compared by direction and percent change only, and magnitudes were reported but not scored. The primary analysis used the shipped model with the baroreflex on; the reflex-off model was a sensitivity analysis.

Ejection fraction was defined as native aortic-valve stroke volume divided by LV end-diastolic volume (EDV), applied identically to all scenarios; the total fractional LV emptying, which includes pump outflow, was reported separately as LV emptying fraction. Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) was represented by mean left atrial pressure (LAP).

## Software, data, and AI use
All analyses were scripted; every reported number is written by a script to a results file. The code, verification scripts, extracted benchmark values, and results are available at https://github.com/Rugved3-droid/cardioflow-mcs (tag {{TAG}}, commit {{HASH}}). Claude (Anthropic) was used to write and run the verification and figure scripts, to extract and tabulate values from the benchmark articles, and to draft the manuscript; the authors reviewed the code, checked extracted values against the source articles, and edited and approved the final text. This in silico study involved no human or animal participants.

# RESULTS
## Numerical verification
The headless runner matched the browser engine to a maximum relative difference of 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁴. At the shipped step size, halving h changed no output by more than 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ (relative), well inside the 0.5% criterion. All scenarios reached a periodic steady state 33–63 beats after a device change, and total blood volume drifted by at most 2.2 × 10⁻⁹ mL over 510,000 ticks. In paired runs, the unchanged pane was bit-identical to an independent single run over 1,000 beats in all 9 tests.

## Baseline
The unsupported state had a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 61.9 mmHg, native cardiac output of 2.98 L/min, stroke volume of 29.8 mL, LVEDP of 20.0 mmHg, EDV of 188 mL, native ejection fraction of 15.9%, and mean LAP of 20.2 mmHg (SDC Table S4). Compared with the LV-failure state of the source model,[@donker2019simulation] MAP, cardiac output, stroke volume, and heart rate agreed within 5%, but the CardioFlow LV was 19% larger and left-sided filling pressure was lower (mean LAP 20 vs PCWP 30 mmHg).

## Device effects
VA-ECMO at 4 L/min raised MAP by 19.1 mmHg, LVEDP by 6.5 mmHg, and EDV by 12%, and lowered native stroke volume by 24% (Figures 2 and 4). All outcomes changed monotonically from 0 to 7 L/min. The aortic valve continued to open in every ECMO scenario, for 23% of the cycle at 4 L/min and 20% at 7 L/min; no benchmark reported aortic valve opening, so this output is not benchmarked. Impella P8 without ECMO lowered LVEDP by 7.0 mmHg and abolished native ejection, and the IABP lowered LVEDP by 3.4 mmHg.

On ECMO at 4 L/min, Impella flow rose with P-level from 1.05 L/min at P2 to 3.40 L/min at P8 (Figure 3). Unloading was dose dependent. At P5 (2.2 L/min), the Impella lowered mean LAP by 3.5 mmHg and the IABP by 4.1 mmHg; from P6 (2.6 L/min) upward the Impella unloaded more, reaching −9.7 mmHg at P8. The IABP increased native stroke volume by 22%, whereas the Impella reduced it, to zero from P7 upward. With a 30-mL balloon and the reflex removed, matching the source-model configuration, the IABP lowered mean LAP by 2.4 mmHg, compared with 1 mmHg in the source model. The P5 level used as the default combined-support state in the application had been chosen for display, not physiology.

## Concordance with benchmarks
With the baroreflex on, the model reproduced the direction of 35 of 38 comparisons with consistency sources and 24 of 29 with independent sources, including 10 of 11 comparisons in which the benchmark change was statistically significant (Table 2). Counts were identical with the reflex off. Magnitudes of ECMO-induced changes were close to those reported by Møller-Helgestad et al. for LVEDP (+5.4 vs +5 mmHg) and EDV (+10% vs +10%) and by Hála et al. for end-systolic volume (+20% vs +20%), but the model's LVEDP rise across the ECMO sweep was smaller than in Hála et al. (+35% vs +114%; Figure 2).

Eight comparisons were discordant (Table 3). Stroke work fell with increasing ECMO flow in the model but rose in both porcine sweeps, and stroke volume fell by 28% where Hála et al. found no net change. Mean pulmonary artery pressure rose in the model at ECMO onset but fell in Nix et al. LV peak pressure fell on Impella in the model but rose in Møller-Helgestad et al., whose animals all received norepinephrine. EDV at ECMO onset rose in the model and was described as slightly reduced by Nix et al., whereas the other two independent studies reported increases. The remaining two were the IABP ranking at P5 and an IABP-induced MAP rise where the source model had held MAP fixed by protocol.

## Sensitivity
None of 50 device-effect directions reversed in any of 31 perturbed configurations. Magnitudes were most sensitive to Ees and EDPVR stiffness; for example, the ECMO-induced LVEDP rise ranged from +5.0 to +8.8 mmHg under ±20% Ees. Removing the baroreflex changed no direction (0 of 63) but roughly doubled ECMO-induced changes (MAP +36.5 vs +19.1 mmHg; LVEDP +12.9 vs +6.5 mmHg) and modestly enlarged IABP and Impella unloading on ECMO.

# DISCUSSION
This study shows that a compact, browser-based simulator can be verified to a standard that is reproducible by anyone with the code. The numerical checks leave no meaningful solver error, and the paired-state design isolates one intervention exactly, which is its purpose. The more important result is the benchmark comparison, because it distinguishes what the model was built to reproduce from what it predicts.

Against independent porcine data, the model reproduced the direction of most device effects: LV dilation and rising filling pressure with increasing ECMO flow, falling native output, and greater volume unloading by a transaortic pump than by ECMO.[@hala2020; @mollerhelgestad2019; @frontiers2020impella] Its disagreements form a coherent pattern rather than scattered errors. The simulated ventricle is weak and afterload-sensitive, so it cannot raise its stroke work or hold its stroke volume when ECMO raises afterload, as the porcine ventricles did; this behavior is phenotype-dependent, and the same model produces rising stroke work at higher contractility. The pulmonary pressure and LV peak pressure discrepancies reflect missing physiology, namely RV–pulmonary coupling during right-heart bypass and vasopressor effects.

The Impella–IABP comparison illustrates why dose matters more than device class. Whether balloon pumping or a transaortic pump unloads more on ECMO depended on pump flow, with a crossover near 2.6 L/min; above it the Impella was clearly superior, consistent with the source model and with the direction of the porcine data.[@donker2019simulation; @mollerhelgestad2019] The IABP effect itself was at the upper end of published estimates,[@donker2019review] which we attribute to displacing the full balloon volume against a single arterial compliance without partial occlusion. Reporting the full dose–response avoids presenting a single device setting chosen after the results were known.

The baroreflex is the largest structural uncertainty. It never changed a direction, but it halved the hemodynamic cost of ECMO, and its gain was chosen to reproduce a target from the source model rather than measured. Quantitative statements about the magnitude of ECMO-induced LV loading from this model should therefore be read as reflex-dependent.

## Limitations
The model represents one patient phenotype and was not calibrated to normal physiology. Heart rate is fixed, and the reflex acts only on SVR with an assumed gain. There is no atrial contraction, ventricular interdependence, inertance, valve regurgitation, coronary or regional circulation, or gas exchange, so differential hypoxia cannot be represented. VA-ECMO is an ideal flow source without a pump curve, and its drainage limit was never engaged. The Impella H–Q relation is illustrative, and pump flow on ECMO cannot exceed 3.8 L/min, so conclusions are limited to Impella CP-class support. The IABP is a volume displacement in one compartment. Benchmarks were few, largely porcine, and compared by direction only; several consistency sources informed the model's parameters. The simulator has not been validated for patient-specific decisions.

## Conclusions
CardioFlow Twin is numerically verified, and its device-effect directions agree with most independent porcine benchmarks, with documented disagreements attributable to its single weak-ventricle phenotype and missing physiology. It is suited to reasoning about the direction of Impella CP-class, IABP, and VA-ECMO effects, not to patient-specific prediction.
