# TITLE
Paired-State Simulation of Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support

# ABSTRACT
Simulators of temporary mechanical circulatory support are rarely verified against published data in a way that separates calibration targets from independent evidence. We verified CardioFlow Twin, an open-source browser-based lumped-parameter simulator that runs two identical copies of one cardiogenic-shock patient side by side, differing in one device setting (intra-aortic balloon pump [IABP], Impella-class pump, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [VA-ECMO], or combinations). The solver matched the browser engine to 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁴, time-step halving changed outputs by <1.1 × 10⁻⁶, blood volume drifted ≤2.2 × 10⁻⁹ mL, and paired panes were bit-identical over 1,000 beats. Against three independent porcine studies, the model reproduced the direction of 24 of 29 device effects (10 of 11 statistically significant changes); against its source model and calibration anchors, 35 of 38. No direction reversed under ±20% parameter perturbation or without the baroreflex, although removing the reflex doubled ECMO-induced changes (left ventricular end-diastolic pressure +12.9 vs +6.5 mmHg). On ECMO, Impella unloading exceeded IABP unloading only above 2.6 L/min of pump flow. Disagreements involved stroke work, stroke volume, and pulmonary pressure on ECMO. The simulator supports directional reasoning about Impella CP-class support and is not validated for patient-specific decisions.

# KEYWORDS
cardiogenic shock; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; intra-aortic balloon pump; computer simulation; ventricular unloading

# INTRODUCTION
The hemodynamic effects of temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) are difficult to reason about at the bedside. Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) restores systemic flow but raises left ventricular (LV) afterload, transaortic axial pumps unload the LV directly, and the intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) modestly lowers afterload while augmenting diastolic pressure.[@burkhoff2015] Randomized trials of extracorporeal life support and of a microaxial flow pump in infarct-related shock have reached divergent results,[@thiele2023; @moller2024] which sharpens the need to understand how each device changes LV loading.

Closed-loop simulation has been used to quantify LV overload during VA-ECMO and to compare unloading strategies,[@broome2016; @donker2019simulation; @donker2019review] and such predictions can be checked against large-animal pressure–volume (PV) data.[@ostadal2015; @hala2020; @mollerhelgestad2019; @frontiers2020impella] Simulators are, however, usually reported with their outputs rather than a verification record, and the data used to set a model's parameters are rarely separated from the data used to test it. Agreement with a benchmark the model was tuned toward is consistency, not validation.

CardioFlow Twin displays two copies of one patient side by side, differing in exactly one device setting, so that the effect of a single intervention can be seen without confounding. We report its verification: solver correctness, baseline plausibility, directional concordance of device effects with published benchmarks classified as consistency or independent sources, sensitivity of those directions, and integrity of the paired-state design. Every disagreement is reported.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS
## Model
CardioFlow Twin is a closed-loop lumped-parameter model written in TypeScript and distributed as a single HTML file that runs in a web browser (Figure 1). Seven volume compartments represent the LV, right ventricle (RV), proximal aortic root, systemic arteries, systemic veins, pulmonary arteries, and a combined pulmonary venous–left atrial compartment. Each ventricle is a time-varying elastance with a double-Hill activation function [CITE NEEDED] and an exponential end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR). Valves are ideal diodes. The systemic arteries form a three-element Windkessel with a proximal aortic node [CITE NEEDED], the veins a finite stressed reservoir, and a bronchial shunt links systemic arteries to the pulmonary veins. Heart rate is fixed at 100 bpm. A baroreflex adjusts systemic vascular resistance (SVR) through a sigmoid of sensed mean pressure with 2-s sensing and 8-s effector lags [CITE NEEDED]; heart rate, contractility, and venous tone are not reflex-controlled. Equations and all parameters are in Supplemental Digital Content (SDC) Section S1 and Table S1; key parameters are in Table 1.

The patient is a single phenotype of low-contractility, afterload-dominant LV failure (end-systolic elastance [Ees] 0.62 mmHg/mL, arterial-to-ventricular elastance ratio ≈4, ejection fraction 16%), corresponding to a stage C–D shock profile [CITE NEEDED]. No normal-physiology parameter set was calibrated.

## Devices
The IABP displaces 40 mL into the systemic arterial compartment, inflating at the dicrotic notch and deflating just before aortic valve opening. The Impella-class pump draws from the LV and returns to the aortic root; its flow lies on a linear pressure head–flow (H–Q) line whose shut-off head scales with the square of the performance level (P1–P9), with a suction roll-off near LV collapse. The H–Q constants are illustrative (P2–P8 span roughly 0.5–3.5 L/min), not a manufacturer curve. VA-ECMO is an ideal flow source from the systemic veins to the systemic arteries, with a drainage roll-off at low venous pressure and no pump pressure–flow curve.

## Paired-state design and solver
Two independent instances of the patient share one cardiac clock and differ in one device setting. Equations are integrated with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme (h = 0.25 ms). Each scenario runs 90 s unsupported and then 120 s on the target support; outcomes are taken from the final beat.

## Parameter provenance
Parameters were set by the developers to reproduce a shock profile, and three groups were adjusted toward published targets during development. The LV EDPVR was set so that diastolic stiffness matched the change reported by Donker et al. (+10% end-diastolic volume for +5 mmHg wedge pressure at VA-ECMO 4 L/min).[@donker2019simulation] Ees was raised after comparison with the stroke-work direction reported by Ostadal et al.[@ostadal2015] The baroreflex was added, with a setpoint reproducing the pre-existing SVR, to bring the ECMO-induced rise in LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) into a window taken from Donker et al. The reflex gain is a modeling assumption, not a measurement; the Donker model has no reflexes. No parameter came from the independent sources, and none was changed for this study.

## Verification
Phase 0 tested whether a headless runner importing the same source reproduces the browser engine (tolerance 10⁻⁶). V1 tested time-step convergence (<0.5% change on halving h), arrival at a periodic steady state, and conservation of blood volume. V2 compared the unsupported state with shock baselines in the benchmark sources. V3 compared simulated device effects with benchmarks. V4 perturbed 15 parameters one at a time by ±20%, and removed the baroreflex, to test whether any device-effect direction reversed. V5 tested whether changing one pane alters the other.

## Benchmarks
Benchmarks were open full-text sources that we downloaded and read. The Donker simulation study and review,[@donker2019simulation; @donker2019review] the Broomé simulation study,[@broome2016] and the porcine study of Ostadal et al.[@ostadal2015] are consistency sources. Porcine studies by Hála et al.,[@hala2020] Nix et al. (industry-funded by Abiomed),[@frontiers2020impella] and Møller-Helgestad et al.[@mollerhelgestad2019] were never used in development and are independent. Every value was recorded with its table, figure, or text location (SDC Table S5).

Each comparison scored the direction of change of one outcome for one device contrast matched to the benchmark protocol (e.g., ECMO 1→5 L/min for Ostadal et al.; Impella CP at maximum support mapped to P8), or the sign of a difference between two devices. Rules were fixed in code beforehand: a model change <1% counted as no change, animal data were compared by direction and percent change only, and magnitudes were reported but not scored. The shipped model (baroreflex on) was primary; the reflex-off model was a sensitivity analysis.

Ejection fraction was native aortic-valve stroke volume divided by LV end-diastolic volume (EDV) in all scenarios; fractional LV emptying including pump outflow was reported separately. Mean left atrial pressure (LAP) represented pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP).

## Software, data, and AI use
Every reported number is written by a script to a results file. Code, scripts, extracted benchmark values, and results are at https://github.com/Rugved3-droid/cardioflow-mcs (tag {{TAG}}, commit {{HASH}}). Claude (Anthropic) was used to write and run the verification and figure scripts, to extract values from the benchmark articles, and to draft the manuscript; the authors reviewed the code, checked extracted values against the sources, and edited and approved the text. This in silico study involved no human or animal participants.

# RESULTS
## Numerical verification
The headless runner matched the browser engine to a maximum relative difference of 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁴. Halving h changed no output by more than 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ (relative). Scenarios reached a periodic steady state 33–63 beats after a device change, and blood volume drifted by at most 2.2 × 10⁻⁹ mL over 510,000 ticks. In all 9 paired tests, the unchanged pane was bit-identical to an independent run over 1,000 beats.

## Baseline
Unsupported, mean arterial pressure (MAP) was 61.9 mmHg, native cardiac output 2.98 L/min, stroke volume 29.8 mL, LVEDP 20.0 mmHg, EDV 188 mL, ejection fraction 15.9%, and mean LAP 20.2 mmHg (SDC Table S4). Compared with the LV-failure state of the source model,[@donker2019simulation] MAP, cardiac output, stroke volume, and heart rate agreed within 5%, but the LV was 19% larger and filling pressure lower (mean LAP 20 vs PCWP 30 mmHg).

## Device effects
VA-ECMO at 4 L/min raised MAP by 19.1 mmHg, LVEDP by 6.5 mmHg, and EDV by 12%, and lowered native stroke volume by 24% (Figures 2 and 4); all outcomes changed monotonically from 0 to 7 L/min. The aortic valve opened in every ECMO scenario (23% of the cycle at 4 L/min, 20% at 7 L/min); no benchmark reported valve opening, so this output is unbenchmarked. Without ECMO, Impella P8 lowered LVEDP by 7.0 mmHg and abolished native ejection, and the IABP lowered LVEDP by 3.4 mmHg.

On ECMO at 4 L/min, Impella flow rose from 1.05 L/min at P2 to 3.40 L/min at P8 (Figure 3). At P5 (2.2 L/min), the Impella lowered mean LAP by 3.5 mmHg and the IABP by 4.1 mmHg; from P6 (2.6 L/min) upward the Impella unloaded more, reaching −9.7 mmHg at P8. The IABP increased native stroke volume by 22%, whereas the Impella reduced it, to zero from P7. With a 30-mL balloon and no reflex, matching the source-model configuration, the IABP lowered mean LAP by 2.4 mmHg (source model, 1 mmHg). The application's default combined-support level, P5, had been chosen for display rather than physiology. Because pump outflow empties the LV without aortic valve opening, the two ejection metrics diverged under Impella support: at ECMO 4 L/min with P8, native ejection fraction was 0% while fractional LV emptying was 11.0%.

## Concordance with benchmarks
With the baroreflex on, the model reproduced the direction of 35 of 38 comparisons with consistency sources and 24 of 29 with independent sources, including 10 of 11 in which the benchmark change was statistically significant (Table 2); counts were identical with the reflex off. ECMO-induced changes matched Møller-Helgestad et al. in magnitude for LVEDP (+5.4 vs +5 mmHg) and EDV (+10% vs +10%), and Hála et al. for end-systolic volume (+20% vs +20%), but the LVEDP rise across the ECMO sweep was smaller than in Hála et al. (+35% vs +114%; Figure 2).

Eight comparisons were discordant (Table 3). With increasing ECMO flow, stroke work fell in the model but rose in both porcine sweeps, and stroke volume fell by 28% where Hála et al. found no net change. At ECMO onset, mean pulmonary artery pressure rose in the model but fell in Nix et al., and EDV rose where Nix et al. described a slight reduction and the other independent studies reported increases. On Impella, LV peak pressure fell in the model but rose in Møller-Helgestad et al., whose animals received norepinephrine. The others were the IABP ranking at P5 and a MAP rise with IABP where the source model had held MAP fixed.

## Sensitivity
None of 50 device-effect directions reversed in 31 perturbed configurations. Magnitudes were most sensitive to Ees and EDPVR stiffness; the ECMO-induced LVEDP rise ranged from +5.0 to +8.8 mmHg under ±20% Ees. Removing the baroreflex reversed no direction (0 of 63) but roughly doubled ECMO-induced changes (MAP +36.5 vs +19.1 mmHg; LVEDP +12.9 vs +6.5 mmHg) and modestly enlarged unloading by IABP and Impella on ECMO.

# DISCUSSION
Against independent porcine data, the model reproduced the direction of most device effects: LV dilation and rising filling pressure with ECMO flow, falling native output, and greater volume unloading by a transaortic pump than by ECMO.[@hala2020; @mollerhelgestad2019; @frontiers2020impella] Its disagreements form a pattern rather than scattered errors. The simulated ventricle is weak and afterload-sensitive, so it cannot raise stroke work or hold stroke volume when ECMO raises afterload, as the porcine ventricles did; this behavior is phenotype-dependent. The pulmonary pressure and LV peak pressure discrepancies reflect absent physiology: RV–pulmonary coupling during right-heart bypass, and vasopressors.

The Impella–IABP comparison shows that dose matters more than device class. Whether balloon pumping or a transaortic pump unloaded more on ECMO depended on pump flow, with a crossover near 2.6 L/min, above which the Impella was clearly superior, as in the source model and the porcine data.[@donker2019simulation; @mollerhelgestad2019] The IABP effect was at the upper end of published estimates,[@donker2019review] which we attribute to displacing the whole balloon volume against a single arterial compliance without partial occlusion. Reporting the full dose–response avoids presenting a device setting chosen after the results were known.

The baroreflex is the largest structural uncertainty. It never changed a direction but halved the hemodynamic cost of ECMO, and its gain was chosen to match a source-model target rather than measured. Magnitudes of ECMO-induced LV loading from this model are therefore reflex-dependent.

Directional concordance is a deliberately modest standard. It suits a tool meant to show which way a variable moves when one device setting changes, which is what the paired-state design isolates, but it does not establish quantitative accuracy, and porcine magnitudes cannot be transferred to patients. The next tests are human data that resolve the questions this study could not: serial PV or catheter measurements across ECMO flows and Impella levels, aortic valve opening on ECMO, and support beyond CP-class flows.

## Limitations
The model represents one phenotype and was not calibrated to normal physiology. Heart rate is fixed. There is no atrial contraction, ventricular interdependence, inertance, valve regurgitation, coronary or regional circulation, or gas exchange, so differential hypoxia cannot be represented. VA-ECMO has no pump curve and its drainage limit was never engaged. The Impella H–Q relation is illustrative and pump flow on ECMO cannot exceed 3.8 L/min, so conclusions are limited to Impella CP-class support. The IABP is a volume displacement in one compartment. Benchmarks were few, mostly porcine, and compared by direction only. The simulator has not been validated for patient-specific decisions.

## Conclusions
CardioFlow Twin is numerically verified, and its device-effect directions agree with most independent porcine benchmarks; its disagreements trace to a single weak-ventricle phenotype and absent physiology. It is suited to reasoning about the direction of IABP, VA-ECMO, and Impella CP-class effects, not to patient-specific prediction.
